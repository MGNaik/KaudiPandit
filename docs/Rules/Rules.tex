\section*{Why this document}

I'm currently working on a learning project to build an AI agent that plays Kauda Baji called Kaudi-Pandit.
The idea is to build a complete model of the game in code, then design AI agents that can play it.
In the future, time permitting, there may even be a robot that plays the game with you in real life.

I've progressed though making models for the board, player, pieces, and moves.
The next step is defining the rules. This must be completed before moving to designing the AI agent. 

And this is where I need your help!

The ask is for you to please read this document and provide feedback if you agree or disagree with each rule.
Because a computer will be playing using these rules, they must be fully unambiguous.

I would be grateful for your time and feedback.

If you'd like to follow my progress, the code lives at
\url{https://github.com/MGNaik/KaudiPandit}.

\section*{Terms used}

Our game is played in Gujarati, but code is typically written in English so I've had to come up with some terms for common concepts.
Here are the terms I will use in this document, and their meanings.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1em, style=nextline, itemsep=1pt, parsep=0pt]

  \item[Piece] One of 6 movable objects belonging to one player.

  \item[Tuple] A player's own pieces stacked on the same square and moving
    together as a unit.

  \item[Tuple Size] The number of pieces in a tuple. A tuple of size $n$ is
    called an $n$-tuple --- a 1-tuple is a single piece on its own, a 2-tuple
    has size 2, a 4-tuple has size 4, etc.

  \item[Kaudi] One of six cowrie shells thrown together to generate a move.
    Plural: \emph{kaudis}.

  \item[Safe square] A square marked with a corner-to-corner cross. The
    starting squares and the centre square are safe.

  \item[Kill] Removing an opponent's tuple from the board. Killed pieces go
    back to their owner's starting square and begin their journey again.

\end{description}

\section*{Overview of the Game}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=0.8444\textwidth]{figures/kauda_baji_board}
\end{minipage}%
\hspace{0.02\textwidth}
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/kaudi_shells_bw.jpg}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

The board is a 7$\times$7 grid of squares. Four players sit at the four edges;
each player owns the edge nearest them.

Moves are generated by throwing a set of six \emph{kaudis} (cowrie shells),
and the outcome of the throw is used to move the players' pieces around the
board.

Each player has six pieces of their own colour.
Every player's pieces start on the same square: the crossed square in the
middle of that player's own edge. This is the player's \textbf{starting
square}. From there, pieces travel around and inward, and all four players
finish on the single square at the very centre of the board.

Along the way, a player may also use their pieces to kill other players'
pieces, sending them back to their starting square --- which can increase
their own chances of winning.

The objective of the game is to get all six pieces from the starting square to the centre square. The first player to do so wins.


\section*{The rules}

\ruletitle{1}{Determining the first player}

Before play begins, every player throws their kaudis once. Whoever rolls the
highest value goes first. Play then proceeds \textbf{anticlockwise} around
the table from there.

\emph{Example:} Red throws 4, Blue throws 6, Green throws 2, Yellow throws
12. Yellow goes first (12 beats everything), then play continues
anticlockwise from Yellow's seat.

\emph{Example:} Red throws 5, Blue throws 5, Green throws 6, Yellow throws 3.
Green goes first with the single highest value, 6.

If two or more players tie for the highest value, only those tied players
throw again to break the tie; whoever wins that re-throw goes first. Players
who weren't tied are not involved in the re-throw.

\emph{Example:} Red throws 6, Blue throws 6, Green throws 4, Yellow throws
2. Red and Blue are tied for highest and throw again, just the two of them,
until one beats the other.

\newpage
\ruletitle{2}{Throwing the kaudis}

A turn begins with a throw of all six kaudis. The value of a throw is the
number of kaudis that land mouth-up, except that a throw with \textbf{no}
kaudis mouth-up is worth \textbf{12} rather than 0. So a single throw is
worth 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, or 12.

A throw of 6 or 12 earns the player an extra throw, and the value of that
extra throw is \textbf{banked} alongside the first. If a player throws 6 or
12 three times in a row, they forfeit the rest of their turn --- none of the
banked values may be used.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/kaudi_throw_examples}
\end{center}

\emph{Example:} A player throws 6 (a bonus), throws again and gets 4 --- since
4 isn't 6 or 12, the turn stops there with two values banked to spend: 6 and
4. A player who instead throws 6, then 12, then 6 has hit a bonus three
times in a row and forfeits the whole turn --- none of those three values
may be used.

\ruletitle{3}{Using a throw to move}

Each throw value produced during a turn moves one piece that many squares. 
It is the player's decision which pieces they allocate throw values to.

If a turn produces more than one value because it included one or two 
throws of 6 or 12 (see Rule 2), the total value may be spent on a 
single piece (added together), or spread across several different pieces, 
in any combination the player chooses. 

However, a single throw value cannot be split between two pieces.
A throw of 6 and 3 in the same turn, for example, can move one piece 9
squares in total, or two different pieces 6 and 3 squares each,
but the 6 and the 3 can never themselves be divided further.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule3_throw_moves}\\[1mm]
  {\small A turn produced 6 and 3. The player spends the 6 on one piece and the 3 on
  another, each advancing along the route --- or could have moved a single piece
  9 squares in total.}
\end{center}

Throws that can legally be used by any of the player's pieces must be used, there
is no option to forfeit or skip other than two special cases in Rule 9 and Rule 16.


\emph{Example:} A player throws 6 (a bonus, so they throw again), then 12
(another bonus, so they throw once more), then 4 --- not a 6 or 12, so the
turn stops there with three values banked: 6, 12, and 4. Legal ways to
spend them include: one piece moves 22 (6+12+4); one piece moves 18
(6+12) and a second piece moves 4; or three different pieces move 6, 12,
and 4 respectively. Not legal: splitting the 6 into a 4-and-2 to feed two
pieces.

\emph{Example:} A player throws 5 on their first throw. Since 5 is neither
6 nor 12, no bonus throw follows --- the turn produces just that one value.
The whole 5 must go to a single piece; there's nothing to spread, since
there's only the one value.

\newpage
\ruletitle{4}{The movement path: anticlockwise out, clockwise in, three arrows to the centre}

Every piece follows the same route from its starting square to the centre.
The route is identical for all four players, just rotated to begin at each
player's own edge.

A piece begins on its \textbf{starting square} --- the crossed square in the
middle of the player's edge --- and travels anticlockwise around the outer
ring of the board. At the \textbf{first arrow}, it turns inward onto the
first inner band and continues \textbf{clockwise} around that band. At the
\textbf{second arrow}, it turns inward again onto the second inner band,
still travelling clockwise. The \textbf{third and final arrow} then carries
the piece onto the centre square, completing its journey.

At each of the three arrows, a piece is not forced inward: its owner may
choose to follow the arrow onto the next band, or keep the piece moving
straight ahead on its current band instead, for strategic reasons. (The
first arrow carries one further condition --- see Rule 5.)

  {\small The full route: anticlockwise on the outer ring, clockwise on
  each inner band, three arrows carrying the piece to the centre.}
\end{minipage}%

\ruletitle{5}{The Markut rule: a kill is required to enter the first inner band}

A player may not turn any piece inward at the \textbf{first} arrow until
that player has killed at least one opposing piece, at any point
earlier in the game. This is a one-off unlock, not a per-piece cost: once a
player has made their first kill, \emph{all} of that player's pieces may
enter the first inner band freely from then on. No kill is required to enter
the second inner band or the centre --- the requirement applies only to the
first arrow.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/markut_rule_board}\\[1mm]
  {\small Arrow 1 is gated by this rule (highlighted yellow); arrows 2 and 3 (dashed grey rings) are not.}
\end{center}

\newpage
\ruletitle{6}{A kill earns a bonus throw}

Making a kill --- landing on and killing an opponent (Rule 11 or 13), or
killing on exit (Rule 12) --- earns the killer an immediate extra throw.
Besides this and throwing 6 or 12 (Rule 2), nothing else grants a bonus
throw.

A bonus throw earned from a kill does \textbf{not} count towards the limit
in Rule 2 of forfeiting after rolling 6 or 12 three times in a row --- that
limit is only about consecutive 6s and 12s \emph{from the kaudis
themselves}. A kill's bonus throw doesn't reset or extend that count either
way.

\ruletitle{7}{Tuples form only when a player chooses to move pieces off together}

When one of a player's pieces or tuples lands on a square already holding
one or more of that \textbf{same} player's own pieces or tuples, nothing
merges automatically --- they simply share the square as separate pieces,
exactly as before. A tuple only forms at the moment its owner chooses to
move two or more of them off that square \textbf{together}, as a single
unit; forming and moving happen in the same action, and that move must obey
the divide rule (Rule 9) for the combined size. If the pieces are instead
moved off individually, or not moved together, no tuple forms.

Once formed, a tuple moves, and is killed, together as one unit (see
\emph{Tuple} in Terms used). Tuple size is a plain sum of the pieces merged
into it, with no cap other than a player's own six pieces --- two dugalu
(2-tuples) can combine into a chogalu (4-tuple), and so on up to a single
6-tuple holding all of a player's pieces at once.

A tuple can form on any square other than a player's own \textbf{starting
square} --- ordinary squares and safe squares alike. The starting square is
the one exception: pieces waiting there remain single pieces no matter how
many share it, and can never be moved off together as a tuple from there. A
tuple can only begin forming once a piece has left the starting square.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.8357\textwidth]{figures/tuple_forming}
\end{center}

\ruletitle{8}{Tuples may only dissolve on a safe square}

A tuple may split, but only while it occupies a safe square. The split need
not go all the way down to single pieces --- a tuple may divide into any
combination of smaller tuples and pieces, with some parts leaving the square
and others staying behind. Everywhere else on the board, a tuple must move
and be killed as a single unit until it next reaches a safe square.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.8357\textwidth]{figures/tuple_dissolve}
\end{center}

\ruletitle{9}{Moving a tuple: the divide rule}

A tuple of size $n$ can only be moved using a throw value that $n$ divides
without remainder. A 2-tuple needs a throw of 2, 4, 6, or 12; a 4-tuple needs
a throw of 4 or 12; a lone piece (a 1-tuple) can move on any throw, since 1
divides everything. A throw value that doesn't divide evenly by a tuple's
size simply cannot be used to move that tuple.

When a tuple \emph{does} move, it doesn't advance by the full throw value ---
it advances by the throw value \textbf{divided by} its size. A 2-tuple
moved with a throw of 6 advances 3 squares (6~$\div$~2), not 6; a 4-tuple
moved with a throw of 12 advances 3 squares (12~$\div$~4). The bigger the
tuple, the less distance the same throw actually covers.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule9_divide_board}\\[1mm]
  {\small A throw of 6 moves this 2-tuple only 3 squares along the route (6~$\div$~2) ---
  the whole tuple advances together, covering half the distance a single piece would.}
\end{center}

If the player has no usable throw values under this rule, they forfeit the
throw value and their turn is over.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\textbf{Tuple size} & \textbf{Usable throw values} & \textbf{Squares moved} \\
\hline
1-tuple (lone piece) & 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12 --- any throw & same as the throw \\
2-tuple & 2, 4, 6, 12 & 1, 2, 3, 6 \\
3-tuple & 3, 6, 12 & 1, 2, 4 \\
4-tuple & 4, 12 & 1, 3 \\
5-tuple & 5 --- only an exact throw of 5 works & 1 \\
6-tuple & 6, 12 & 1, 2 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\ruletitle{10}{Safe squares: no kills, ever}

Twelve squares carry a corner-to-corner cross, and one more sits at the
centre --- these are the \textbf{safe squares}, marked on the board. Every
other square is ordinary.

No kill of any kind can happen on a safe square. Any number of tuples, of any
sizes, from any players, can share a safe square. Landing there kills nothing,
and leaving a shared safe square kills nothing.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule4_safe_square}
\end{center}

\newpage
\ruletitle{11}{Landing on an opponent: equal-or-bigger kills, smaller coexists}

If your tuple lands on a square holding an opponent's tuple, and your tuple is
the \textbf{same size or bigger}, the opponent's tuple is killed. Your tuple
takes the square; their pieces go back to their start.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule1_landing_kill}
\end{center}

The same happens if the landing tuple is strictly bigger --- a Red~3 landing
on a Blue~2 kills it just the same.

If instead your tuple lands on a square holding an opponent's tuple and
yours is \textbf{strictly smaller}, nothing is killed. Both tuples share the
square.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule2_coexist}
\end{center}

Notice what this means: whenever two opponents share a square, the tuple that
was there first is always the bigger one.

\ruletitle{12}{Leaving a shared square: the bigger tuple kills on exit}

When two opponents share a square and the \textbf{strictly bigger} tuple moves
away, it kills the smaller tuple as it leaves. (This rule is for exactly two
parties sharing a square, each with a single tuple --- see Rule 14 if that
one opponent has multiple separate tuples there instead, or Rule 15 when a
third or later opponent is involved.)

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule3a_exit_kill}
\end{center}

The reverse is safe: if the \emph{smaller} tuple moves away first, no kill
happens --- it simply escapes.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule3b_escape}
\end{center}

The smaller tuple sits under
threat until either it leaves, or the bigger tuple leaves and kills it on the
way out.

\ruletitle{13}{Landing on an opponent with multiple separate tuples: the largest one you can beat dies}

An opponent can have more than one of their own tuples sitting on the same
square without them being merged (see Rule 7) --- for example a 1-tuple and
a 3-tuple side by side, never moved together. When your tuple lands on that
square, look at all of their tuples that are of \textbf{equal or lesser
size} than yours --- the ones you're big enough to beat --- and kill only
the \textbf{largest} of those. Every other tuple of theirs is safe: any of
theirs smaller than the one you killed are spared, and any of theirs bigger
than yours were never at risk to begin with.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.8357\textwidth]{figures/rule_multi_tuple}
\end{center}

This is still a \textbf{two-player} interaction --- one opponent owning
several tuples, not several different opponents (Rule 15 covers that case).
This rule is specifically about \textbf{landing} --- see Rule 14 for what
happens when someone leaves a square like this instead.

\ruletitle{14}{Leaving a square where multiple tuples remain: the same largest-you-can-beat logic}

When a tuple leaves a square, it can kill the \textbf{largest} of the
\textbf{opposing} tuples left behind (belonging to a different player) that
is \textbf{strictly smaller} than the leaving tuple --- matching Rule 12,
where only a \emph{strictly bigger} tuple kills on exit (a tie never
kills). Every opposing tuple that's the same size as the leaver or bigger
is safe, and any opposing tuple smaller than the one killed is spared too.
This applies no matter \textbf{which} tuple leaves --- yours, or one of an
opponent's own separate tuples --- and no matter how many tuples are left
behind.

This is a generalisation of Rule 12, not a separate mechanic: with only one
tuple left behind, ``the largest remaining tuple strictly smaller than the
leaver'' is just that one tuple if it qualifies, so Rule 12 is the
two-tuple special case of this rule.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.8571\textwidth]{figures/rule_multi_tuple_leaving}
\end{center}

\ruletitle{15}{Three or more players on one square: no kills at all, landing or leaving}

Rules 11--14 are only for a square shared by exactly \textbf{one}
opponent (whether that opponent has one tuple there or several unmerged
ones). Once a square already holds tuples from \textbf{two or more
different} opponents, everything changes, for \textbf{both landing and
leaving}: \textbf{no kill can happen there, regardless of size}, in either
direction.

\textbf{Landing:} a tuple landing on such a square simply joins the pile, no
matter how big or small it is compared to what's already there.

\begin{center}
  \includesvg[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/rule5_pairwise}
\end{center}

\textbf{Leaving:} if a tuple currently shares a square with \textbf{two or
more} other opponents' tuples and moves away, it kills nobody on exit
either. Exit kills (Rule 12) only ever happen when exactly one opponent's
tuple is present.

\ruletitle{16}{Reaching the centre: exact throw required}

Sending a piece or tuple on to the centre square, and thus off the board,
requires an \textbf{exact} throw value which lands the piece or tuple onto
the centre square.

A throw of 6 or 12 always earns an immediate extra throw (Rule 2), even
when that 6 or 12 itself overshoots and can't bring a piece home. A later
throw in that same chain --- alone or summed with earlier ones --- can
still complete the piece's journey if it lands exactly; if instead it falls
short, it is not forced onto this piece and follows the same cases below.

As an outcome of this rule, a number of notable cases arise:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Player throws the exact value required to land the piece or tuple on the centre square}

    The piece or tuple lands precisely on the centre and is taken off the
    board --- its journey is complete.

    \emph{Example:} A piece is exactly 2 squares away from the centre. The
    player throws 2 --- the 2 lands the piece precisely on the centre,
    taking it off the board.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case0_exact}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example:} One piece is 2 away and another is 6 away. The player
    throws 6, earning a bonus throw, and the bonus comes up 2 --- the
    values can be used to land both pieces exactly on the centre, and the
    player is free to assign either value to either piece.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case0_choice}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example:} A single piece is 4 away. The player throws 6 --- too
    big to land this piece --- but the 6 earns a bonus throw (Rule 2), and
    the bonus comes up 4. The 4 lands the piece exactly on the centre,
    taking it home; the 6 itself is spent as the cases below describe.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case0_bonus}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Player throws a value smaller than the required value to land the piece or tuple on the centre square}
  
    In this case, the player doesn't have the roll value to land the piece/tuple on the centre.
    However, the lesser move is still an ordinary, legal move, and must be used as per Rule 3. 

    \emph{Example:} A single piece is 4 squares away from the centre and the player has no other pieces on the board. 
    A throw of 3 falls short of landing the piece onto the centre square, but the move is still legal. 
    Since no other piece on the board can use that 3, this piece must be advanced with it.
    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case1_smaller}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example:} A 2-tuple is 5 squares away from the centre and a single piece is 8 squares away.
    A throw of 6 falls short of the 10 needed to land the 2-tuple 
    or the 8 needed to land the single piece on the centre, 
    but the throw is still usable. The player must use the throw
    value and decide which of their pieces or tuples to move.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case1b_tuple_short}
    \end{center}


  \item \textbf{Player throws a value larger than the required value to land the piece or tuple on the centre square}

    In this case, the player has a roll value that is larger than that required to land the piece or tuple
    on the center. The larger throw value cannot be used to land the piece or tuple on the centre square.

    Instead, the player has these options, and is free to choose between
    whichever of them are available --- neither is forced over the other:

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
      \item Move the piece or tuple around the inner band again instead of
        turning in to the centre square, as per Rule 4. This is always a
        legal, ordinary move, whether or not any other piece can use the
        value.
      \item If a different one of the player's pieces or tuples can legally
        use the throw value without overshooting, move that piece or tuple
        with it instead.
    \end{enumerate}

    If no different piece or tuple can use the value --- so option (b)
    isn't available --- the player is not obliged to take option (a)
    either, and may instead forfeit the throw value and finish their turn.

    \emph{Example:} A piece is 2 away. A throw of 5 is too big to land it
    --- it simply cannot be used to move this piece onto the centre.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case2_larger_blocked}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example --- option (a):} The player may spend the 5 on the
    2-away piece itself: declining the arrow (Rule 4), it loops around its
    inner band and ends up 5 away from the centre, waiting for an exact
    value on a later turn.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case2b_loop}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example --- option (b):} Alternatively, with that same throw of
    5, the player also has a second piece out on the first inner band.
    That piece can legally move 5 squares without overshooting --- turning
    in at the second arrow along the way --- so the player may choose to
    move it instead; the 2-away piece is simply left waiting.

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case3_different_piece}
    \end{center}

    \emph{Example --- forfeit:} If instead no different piece anywhere can
    use the 5 (option (b) unavailable), the player isn't obliged to take
    option (a) either --- they may simply forfeit the value and end their
    turn, the same way values are lost when a triple bonus forfeits a turn
    (Rule 2).

    \begin{center}
      \includesvg[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/rule16_case4_forfeited}
    \end{center}
\end{enumerate}
